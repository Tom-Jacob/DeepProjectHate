\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation and problem statement}
\label{sec:motivation}

Forums are open by design, and that openness is what lets a first-hand account reach an
audience it would otherwise never have. It is also what makes them exploitable, and the
resulting abuse is not evenly distributed: it concentrates on ethnic and religious
minorities, people with disabilities, LGBTQ+ users and women, who pay for it by being
driven out of a space they had an equal claim to. Moderating by hand does not scale, so
classifiers are deployed either to rank likely violations for a human or to auto-moderate
with no human in the loop - roles that tolerate errors very differently, since a sorting
tool can afford false alarms and an auto-moderator cannot. Both are usually reported with
a single benchmark number. Three difficulties make that number misleading:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Class imbalance.} Only $\sim$10\% of Jigsaw comments are toxic, so ``never
toxic'' scores 90\% accuracy while detecting nothing.
\item \textbf{Context outside the input.} \emph{``You've wasted enough of everyone's time
here. Give it up.''} closes a dispute when an administrator writes it to a serial vandal,
and drives off a newcomer when three regulars write it in sequence to someone who merely
disagreed. The text is identical; the speaker and the history are not, and neither is in
the input.
\item \textbf{Adversarial users.} Evaders write \texttt{s7up1d} or \texttt{s t u p i d}. A
model whose features are surface tokens breaks immediately, and sub-word tokenization is
only a partial defense.
\end{itemize}

This report is organized around \emph{which} error a model makes rather than how many. A
false negative is a safety failure - \emph{``Shut up you m0r0n''} reaching its target - and
a false positive is a fairness failure - \emph{``I am a gay man and I edited this page''}
removed as abuse. The two are measured by different probes and carry different costs.
\subsection{What this project does}
\label{sec:overview}

We audit rather than rank. Eight configurations spanning four families
(Table~\ref{tab:models}) - a specialized BERT encoder, a TF-IDF/linear-SVM baseline with
no network at all, a prompted and then fine-tuned DistilGPT-2 decoder, and a distilled
DistilBERT student - are evaluated through a single interface, each returning
$P(\text{toxic})$, on one seeded test subset and the same hardware. The eighth
configuration is the control: \texttt{bert-base-uncased} used two ways, fine-tuned with a
classification head and prompted zero-shot through its own masked-LM head.
\texttt{toxic-bert} against DistilGPT-2 changes the decision mechanism and the network
type at once, so a win tells you nothing about which one won; the two
\texttt{bert-base-uncased} configurations share every pretrained weight, leaving the
mechanism as the only variable and therefore the only thing a gap between them can be
blamed on. Each model then goes through three probes - accuracy versus cost, a
368-sentence identity probe on which every positive is by construction a false positive,
and a perturbation suite (\S\ref{sec:setup}) - plus a masking intervention that asks
where the bias sits.

\textbf{Hypotheses.} \textbf{H1.} Identity bias is learned from labeled task data, so the
model specialized on it over-flags neutral identity mentions more than a non-neural
baseline trained on the same data. \textbf{H2.} Distillation preserves the teacher's
accuracy but amplifies its identity bias, the student having fewer parameters with which
to represent the exceptions. \textbf{H3.} A trained discriminative head beats zero-shot
prompting at this scale, independently of whether the network is an encoder or a decoder.
\S\ref{sec:results} reports the numbers.

\subsection{Prior work}

The Jigsaw Toxic Comment Classification Challenge~\cite{jigsaw2018} established the
dataset and the multi-label formulation. Dixon et al.~\cite{dixon2018} introduced
identity-mention templates for measuring unintended bias, the methodology our fairness
probe follows. \texttt{toxic-bert} is Unitary's Detoxify release~\cite{detoxify}; BERT
and its masked-language pretraining objective are due to Devlin et
al.~\cite{devlin2019}, the distilled variants to Sanh et al.~\cite{sanh2019}, and the
distillation objective itself to Hinton et al.~\cite{hinton2015}. We build on these by
comparing an encoder classifier, a non-neural baseline and decoder prompting under a
single protocol, and by adding a perturbation stress test, a masking intervention, and a
same-checkpoint control.

\subsection{One checkpoint, three configurations}
\label{sec:onearch}

Three of our configurations are literally the same network: a $12$-layer transformer
encoder, width $768$, $12$ heads, $109$M parameters, one $30{,}522$-token WordPiece
vocabulary.
Swapping one for another changes no code. What differs is \textbf{which training stages were run, and on what data}: (1)
\emph{generic pretraining} - masked-word prediction on Wikipedia and
\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BookCorpus}{BookCorpus}, producing
\texttt{bert-base-uncased}~\cite{devlin2019}; and (2) \emph{supervised fine-tuning} - a
classification head and the encoder beneath it, trained together on labelled task data.
Framing the comparison this way is what makes \S\ref{sec:results} interpretable:
differences between our encoders are attributable to specific training stages rather than
to ``being a bigger model''.

\subsection{The encoder architecture, and what the three configurations do with it}

All three encoder configurations run the identical network: WordPiece tokenisation
over a $30{,}522$-token vocabulary with \texttt{[CLS]} prefixed, twelve transformer
blocks of multi-head self-attention and position-wise FFN, and one contextual vector
$h\in\mathbb{R}^{768}$ per position. Two properties carry weight later. Sub-word
splitting means an unknown word never becomes ``unknown'' - \texttt{stuuupiiid}
merely breaks into more pieces than \texttt{stupid} (\S\ref{sec:robust}). And
attention is \textbf{bidirectional}: every token's representation is built from the
whole comment, which is what makes a masked position readable as a summary of it.

Nothing in that stack produces a decision, so a head is attached on top to map one
position's vector to what the task needs - and the head, not the encoder, is what differs
across the three configurations (Fig.~\ref{fig:branch}).

\textbf{The head BERT ships with.} Stage-1 pretraining trained a \emph{masked-LM head}: a
linear layer $\mathbb{R}^{768}\to\mathbb{R}^{30522}$, one logit per vocabulary word, used
to predict tokens hidden during training. Predicting a masked word is a $30{,}522$-way
classification, not generation - no sampling and no decoder are involved.

\textbf{E1-p keeps that head.} No new parameters, no gradient step, no labels. We wrap the
comment in a template ending in \texttt{[MASK]}, run the encoder, and read the pretrained
head's logits at the masked position - but only two of the $30{,}522$, for one toxic and
one benign filler word $v_+,v_-$:
\begin{equation}
P(\text{toxic}\mid x)=\frac{\exp \ell_{v_+}}{\exp \ell_{v_+}+\exp \ell_{v_-}},
\qquad \ell_v = \log p_\theta\bigl(v \mid \text{prompt}(x)\bigr).
\label{eq:mlmprompt}
\end{equation}

\textbf{E1 discards it} and attaches a randomly-initialized linear head $z = Wh+b$ on the
\texttt{[CLS]} vector - $769$ parameters on top of $109$M - which is meaningless until
stage 3 trains it together with every weight beneath it. Jigsaw's labels are not mutually
exclusive (a comment can be both an insult and a threat), so the $K$ outputs are
independent sigmoids rather than a softmax, and the objective is the mean binary
cross-entropy:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}} = -\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}
\Bigl[y_k \log \sigma(z_k) + (1-y_k)\log\bigl(1-\sigma(z_k)\bigr)\Bigr].
\label{eq:bce}
\end{equation}

\textbf{E2 is the same procedure at a different scale}: the identical checkpoint with the
same style of head, fine-tuned by Unitary on the full dataset with GPU resources and
hyper-parameter search, and with $K=6$ rather than $K=1$ - one sigmoid per Jigsaw label.
In Jigsaw \texttt{toxic} is the umbrella label and the other five refine it, so we read
$P(\text{toxic})=\sigma(z_{\texttt{toxic}})$ directly rather than OR-ing the sub-labels
together. E1 is trained on that same umbrella label with $K=1$, so E1 and E2 answer the
identical question and their scores are directly comparable. The full six are used only in
distillation (\S\ref{sec:kd}), where the student matches the teacher's soft outputs on all
labels.



\subsection{The decoder architecture, and what the three prompting variants do with it}

\textbf{What GPT-2 computes, and how it differs from BERT.} DistilGPT-2, the
distilled variant of GPT-2~\cite{radford2019}, is built from the
same transformer blocks, but its attention is \textbf{causal}: a token may attend only to tokens \textbf{before it}, never after. That restriction is what makes it a language model - it can
be trained to predict the next token at every position simultaneously, giving
$p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})$. Two consequences matter here. First, the representation at the final position summaries only the text to its left, so there is no \texttt{[CLS]}-style
slot that sees the whole input at once. Second, the only head it ships with is a next-token head over the vocabulary. \textbf{There is no classification head anywhere in the model}. DistilGPT-2
is the distilled $6$-layer, $82$M-parameter variant.

To extract a decision without a classifier head, we're using the exact format as in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mlmprompt}, but at the next-token position rather than a masked one: append a question to the comment, take the two vocabulary logits corresponding to the two possible answers, and softmax over that pair. The three variants differ only in the prompt and in whether any training occurred.

\textbf{D1 asks directly.} The comment is followed by a yes/no question, and we compare $\ell_{\texttt{yes}}$ against $\ell_{\texttt{no}}$ at the next-token position. Zero-shot - no training of any kind.

\textbf{D2 reframes it as completion.} Instead of a question, the prompt ends mid-sentence so that the natural continuation is a label word, and we compare those two words' logits. Same model, same weights, different phrasing - which is what makes the D1/D2 pair a measure of prompt sensitivity rather than of capability.
To complete this part, we introduce also D1-ft -  It fine-tunes D1's prompt format with causal cross-entropy restricted to the answer token:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = -\log p_\theta\bigl(a^{\star}\mid \mathrm{prompt}(x)\bigr),
\qquad a^{\star}\in\{\texttt{yes},\texttt{no}\},
\end{equation}
so the model is told which answer is correct without any architectural change: still a causal decoder, still answering through vocabulary logits, still no classification head. This separates ``decoders cannot do this'' from ``this decoder was never told what to do''.

Because the context window is $1024$ tokens, prompts are truncated from the left, preserving the tail on which the next-token prediction conditions - truncating from the right would remove the question itself.

\subsection{Non-neural floor (E0)}
E0 uses no neural network at all. A comment becomes a $50$k-entry vector of TF-IDF-weighted
counts over single words and adjacent pairs, so \emph{shut up} carries weight that neither
\emph{shut} nor \emph{up} does alone. Word order beyond two neighbours, grammar and context
are discarded, and every entry is an exact string match, so \emph{an idiot} and \emph{an
id10t} are unrelated features. That vector feeds a linear SVM, trained under the squared-hinge
objective with $L_2$ regularization,
$\min_{w,b}\tfrac12\|w\|_2^2 + C\sum_i \max\bigl(0,1-y_i(w^{\top}x_i+b)\bigr)^2$.
The SVM outputs a signed distance from its decision boundary rather than a probability, so we
fit Platt scaling $P(\text{toxic}\mid x)=\sigma\bigl(a\,(w^{\top}x+b)+c\bigr)$ on held-out data
so that $0.5$ corresponds to a genuine coin-flip case.

Its features are surface tokens, which makes it an interpretable probe: whatever E0 achieves
is, by construction, achievable without any contextual understanding. It also predicts its own
failure mode - change the surface form and the feature vanishes, which is what
\S\ref{sec:robust} measures.

\subsection{Knowledge distillation (S1)}

S1 is a $6$-layer DistilBERT student trained against E2's soft outputs. With per-label
temperature-softened teacher and student probabilities $p^{T}_{t,k}=\sigma(z_{t,k}/T)$ and
$p^{T}_{s,k}=\sigma(z_{s,k}/T)$, the objective mixes a distillation term with the ordinary
supervised term:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}
= \alpha\,T^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{KL}\bigl(p^{T}_{t,k}\,\Vert\,p^{T}_{s,k}\bigr)
+ (1-\alpha)\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}},
\qquad T=2,\ \alpha=0.7 .
\label{eq:kd}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathrm{KL}(\cdot\Vert\cdot)$ is the
\href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kullback-Leibler_divergence}{Kullback-Leibler
divergence}. Since each of the $K$ heads
is an independent sigmoid, the term is a per-label binary KL between the two-outcome
distributions $\{p,1-p\}$, averaged over labels.

The temperature $T$ softens both distributions before comparing them, so the student is
penalized for disagreeing about near-misses and not only about confident predictions. Because softening by $T$ shrinks the gradients by roughly $1/T^{2}$, the $T^{2}$ factor
restores them, keeping the two terms comparably weighted as $T$ varies. Soft targets carry
the teacher's relative confidence between labels, which hard labels discard - the reason
distillation can transfer more than accuracy alone.


\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\subsection{Data and splits}

We use the Jigsaw Toxic Comment Classification Challenge~\cite{jigsaw2018}. Each
Wikipedia talk-page comment carries six binary labels (\texttt{toxic},
\texttt{severe\_toxic}, \texttt{obscene}, \texttt{threat}, \texttt{insult},
\texttt{identity\_hate}); our primary target is binary \texttt{toxic}, with all six
retained for distillation. \texttt{train.csv} is split $85/15$
($135{,}635$ training / $23{,}936$ validation) and the labeled rows of \texttt{test.csv} form the test
set ($63{,}978$).

\subsection{Evaluation process}
\label{sec:protocol}
Every model exposes one interface, \texttt{predict\_toxic\_proba}, returning a
scalar $P(\text{toxic})$ binarised at $0.5$ unless stated otherwise. All headline
results are scored on a single seeded $800$-comment test subset on CPU, so accuracy
and latency are measured under identical conditions, and we report parameter count
and latency (ms/comment) alongside every accuracy figure - a comparison that
ignores cost is not a fair comparison.


\textbf{Metrics.} We report:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{precision} $=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}{+}\mathrm{FP}}$ and \textbf{recall} $=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}{+}\mathrm{FN}}$, whose harmonic mean is \textbf{F1} is our primary metric because it punishes sacrificing either side (balancing FP and FN).
    \item \textbf{PR-AUC}, which suits imbalanced data by focusing on the rare positive class
    \item \textbf{ROC-AUC} as a threshold-independent measure of ranking quality
    \item The false-positive rate $FPR=\frac{\mathrm{FP}}{\mathrm{FP}{+}\mathrm{TN}}$, the quantity of interest in the fairness probe
\end{itemize}
We deliberately \textbf{do not report} accuracy: at ${\sim}10\%$ positives
an all-negative classifier scores $90\%$ accuracy and F1 $=0$.

\subsection{Perturbation suite}

We take comments the model already flags as toxic and apply surface noise that a
human still reads as toxic. A comment evades if its score falls below
$0.5$ after perturbation. The evasion rate is the fraction of originally-detected
comments that evade. Higher means more brittle.

Six obfuscations are applied, all preserving human readability: leetspeak
(\texttt{\$7up1d}), space-out (\texttt{s t u p i d}), punct-noise (\texttt{s.t.u.p.i.d}),
typos (adjacent-character swaps in $\sim$10\% of words, \texttt{sutpid}), repeated
characters (\texttt{stuuupiiid}) and upper case (\texttt{STUPID}).

\subsection{Identity fairness probe}

Following Dixon et al.~\cite{dixon2018} we fill neutral template carriers with $21$
identity terms spanning religion, race, orientation, gender and disability, plus two neutral baselines (\texttt{human}, \texttt{normal}) - $23$ fillers across $16$
templates in three length tiers ($10$ short, $3$ medium ${\sim}50$ words, $3$ long ${\sim}140$ words), giving $16 \times 23 = 368$ probe sentences. None is toxic by
construction, so every positive prediction is a false positive. Per-group FPR is compared against $8$ length-matched sentences with no identity term, isolating the
identity mention from the effect of sentence length.

\subsection{Masking intervention}
\label{sec:masking}

\textbf{(a) Inference masking:} replace the identity term in each probe sentence with
\texttt{[MASK]} (encoders) or the empty string (SVM) and re-measure identity FPR - a
collapse to zero means the model was relying on the token itself.
\textbf{(b) Training-time masking (E0 only):} retrain the SVM on data with identity
terms removed and compare identity FPR and \texttt{identity\_hate} recall against the
unmasked baseline.

\subsection{Robustness to obfuscation}
\label{sec:robust}


We measure robustness in \textbf{both directions}: perturbing genuinely toxic
comments to see what slips through (\emph{evasion}), and perturbing genuinely
neutral comments to see what gets wrongly flagged (\emph{false-positive
induction}). Reporting only the first, as is common, gives a misleading picture -
as \S\ref{sec:direction} shows.

\texttt{toxic-bert} is by far the hardest to evade (leetspeak $0.280$, space-out
$0.054$, punct-noise $0.150$). The SVM is trivially defeated by every
character-level perturbation ($\approx 0.75$), exactly as its feature space
predicts: change the surface token and the feature disappears. The informative
result is E1 - identical architecture, parameter count and pretraining to
\texttt{toxic-bert}, yet nearly as brittle as the bag of words ($0.719$ vs.\
$0.711$ leetspeak evasion). Sub-word tokenisation alone therefore does not confer
robustness; \texttt{toxic-bert}'s advantage tracks its specific large-scale
in-domain fine-tune, not the fact that it is a BERT.

\textbf{Distillation transferred fairness but not robustness.} S1 is the sharpest
new result here. It reproduces its teacher's \emph{clean-text} behaviour well
(\S\ref{sec:kd}) and improves on its fairness by an order of magnitude, yet it is
more than twice as easy to evade: leetspeak $0.280 \to 0.634$, punct-noise
$0.150 \to 0.524$, with baseline detection falling $0.930 \to 0.820$. On evasion
it sits beside the bag-of-words model rather than beside its own teacher. The
plausible mechanism is in Eq.~\eqref{eq:kd} itself: the KD loss matches teacher
logits on \emph{unperturbed} training comments, so the student inherits the
teacher's decision boundary on clean text without inheriting whatever makes that
boundary hard to move. It learns what the teacher concludes, not why. We cannot
fully separate this from under-training, since our student is the early-stopped
checkpoint of \S\ref{sec:kd}; that separation needs the full-scale run.

\subsection{Robustness is direction-dependent}
\label{sec:direction}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.68\textwidth]{figures/robustness_fp_induction_heatmap.png}
\caption{False-positive induction: the fraction of correctly-classified
\emph{neutral} comments that flip to ``toxic'' under each perturbation. Compare
with Fig.~\ref{fig:robust}, the evasion view of the same suite.}
\label{fig:fpinduction}
\end{figure}

Reading Table~\ref{tab:evasion} alone, \texttt{toxic-bert} looks simply robust.
The second direction contradicts that. Applying the \emph{same} perturbations to
neutral comments flips them to ``toxic'' at a rate of $0.910$ (space-out),
$0.809$ (repeated characters) and $0.652$ (punct-noise), against an unperturbed
false-positive rate of only $0.110$. The model that is hardest to trick into
missing real toxicity is simultaneously among the easiest to trick into
manufacturing it.

The SVM shows the mirror image: near-total collapse under evasion, but almost
complete indifference in this direction ($0.021$ for space-out and punct-noise).
It is a \emph{weak but stable} model - never very sensitive to anything unusual,
which hurts it in one direction and helps it in the other. S1 again follows its
teacher here rather than the SVM ($0.707$ space-out, $0.793$ repeated
characters), so the FP-induction weakness \emph{did} survive distillation even
though evasion robustness did not.

The practical consequence is that ``robust'' is not a single property, and a
deployment that reports only evasion resistance is reporting the half of the
picture that flatters it. For an auto-moderator, the FP-induction direction is
arguably the more damaging failure: perturbed neutral text is exactly what
ordinary users produce - emphatic capitalisation, elongated words, stray
punctuation.

\subsection{Masking: one bias, two mechanisms}
\label{sec:maskingresults}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\begin{subfigure}[b]{0.48\textwidth}

\caption{The masking intervention. Left: replacing the identity term with
\texttt{[MASK]} at inference. Right: retraining the SVM with identity terms

\label{fig:masking}
\end{figure}

A deep transformer and a bag-of-words model behave identically here: masking the
identity token collapses both false-positive rates to zero ($0.087\to0$ for E2,
$0.049\to0$ for E0). Finding that clean
lexical shortcut inside \texttt{toxic-bert} is the more interesting half - $109$M
parameters of contextual encoding still route the decision through a single token.
The distilled student follows the same mechanism as its teacher rather than its
own architecture-mate: masking takes it $0.008 \to 0.000$, so the residual bias it
does carry is the same single-token shortcut, merely an order of magnitude
fainter. E1 is the one that breaks the pattern: masking \emph{increases} its
identity FPR ($0.103 \to 0.188$), so ``mask the identity term and the bias goes
away'' is not universal. The likely mechanism is distribution shift - a literal \texttt{[MASK]}
is essentially absent from E1's fine-tuning data, and the out-of-distribution input
pushes this weakly-trained head toward the positive class rather than neutrality.

Training-time masking of the SVM is worse than useless: identity FPR does not fall
($0.049 \to 0.063$) while \texttt{identity\_hate} recall drops sharply
($0.781 \to 0.676$) at roughly unchanged general toxicity recall ($0.659 \to 0.645$).
Deleting the identity vocabulary removes the model's ability to recognise attacks on
those identities without removing its tendency to over-flag them - which is why the
mitigation would have to act on the objective rather than on the vocabulary.

\subsection{Same checkpoint, two methods}
\label{sec:samecheckpoint}

E1 and E1-p hold architecture \emph{and} pretrained weights fixed and vary only
the method. On the same $n{=}800$ subset the trained head dominates on every
metric: F1 $0.675$ vs.\ $0.000$, PR-AUC $0.791$ vs.\ $0.227$, ROC-AUC $0.961$ vs.\
$0.722$. This is the cleanest confirmation of H3 in the project.

The $F1=0$ deserves care: it is a calibration artefact, not an absence of signal.
E1-p's scores almost never cross $0.5$, yet its ROC-AUC of $0.722$ sits far above the
zero-shot decoders' ${\approx}0.4$ - it \emph{ranks} meaningfully and is merely
mis-thresholded. Two conclusions follow: naive thresholding can hide real
discriminative ability entirely, and BERT's bidirectional masked-LM objective gives a
much stronger zero-shot signal than GPT-2's causal one.

The two methods agree on $82.8\%$ of comments. Accuracy against gold looks
deceptively close ($0.908$ vs.\ $0.885$), but E1-p's is mostly the ${\sim}88$-$90\%$
non-toxic base rate; of the $138$ disagreements, E1 consistently catches real
toxicity E1-p misses.

Finally, this pair sharpens H1: generic E1 (identity FPR $0.103$) is \emph{less} fair
than specialised \texttt{toxic-bert} ($0.087$) with architecture family held fixed, so
the naive reading ``specialisation costs fairness'' does not survive. What
distinguishes \texttt{toxic-bert} is the scale and quality of its supervised stage,
and that same stage appears to drive both its fairness and its robustness advantage.

\section{Discussion and limitations}

\textbf{Is the comparison fair?} A fine-tuned encoder against a zero-shot decoder is an
obvious objection, but E0 and S1 are trained on the same data, D1-ft gives the decoder
the same kind of supervision and still trails both encoders and the SVM, and
\texttt{toxic-bert} was trained with more data and tuning than we could apply - so the
comparison is biased \emph{toward} the encoder and our conclusions survive that bias.

\textbf{Limitations.} Headline numbers use an $800$-comment subset rather than the
full $63{,}978$-row test set, chosen so every model - including the
$650$\,ms/comment decoders - could be scored under identical CPU conditions;
confidence intervals at this $n$ are non-trivial and we do not report them.
Two further caveats follow from the same compute constraint. First, S1 and D1-ft
are \emph{early-stopped} checkpoints (\S\ref{sec:kd}): matching the intended
$20$k-row schedule would have cost ${\approx}25$ hours of CPU per checkpoint, so
their accuracy numbers are a floor, not the method's ceiling - and the striking
S1 robustness result in \S\ref{sec:robust} cannot be cleanly separated from
under-training without that run. Second, the perturbation suite is measured at
$n{=}100$ for every model except D1-ft, which is at $n{=}25$; that row indicates
a direction rather than a precise rate.
Table~\ref{tab:kd} comes from separate GPU passes at $n{=}500$ and $n{=}200$, not yet
re-scored on the shared subset. The fairness probe is synthetic: it measures
over-flagging of neutral identity mentions, one facet of fairness and no substitute
for per-group evaluation on real annotated text. All evaluation is in-domain Wikipedia talk-page text. Our proposal also listed
\texttt{GroNLP/hateBERT}~\cite{caselli2021} - \texttt{bert-base} given continued
masked-LM pretraining on abusive-language text - which would have separated
\texttt{toxic-bert}'s advantage into domain exposure versus fine-tuning scale; it was not
run within the project timeframe, so conclusion 2 attributes that advantage to the
supervised stage without ruling out the pretraining domain.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Toxicity detection is substantially lexical, but not purely so.} A
$50$k-feature linear model reaches ${\sim}93\%$ of a $109$M encoder's F1 at
${\sim}1750\times$ lower latency; scale buys a bounded, not transformative, gain.

\item \textbf{The encoder's real value is robustness, and it is earned rather than
architectural.} Our generic \texttt{bert-base-classifier} - identical architecture
and parameter count to \texttt{toxic-bert} - is nearly as brittle to leetspeak as
the linear SVM, and is also \emph{less} fair than the specialised model with
architecture held fixed. Both advantages point at \texttt{toxic-bert}'s training
scale rather than at specialisation or capacity per se.

\item \textbf{A discriminative head beats prompting, most cleanly on a single
checkpoint.} E1 dominates E1-p on every metric using identical pretrained weights,
yet BERT's zero-shot prompt still ranks far better than GPT-2's - architecture and
method contribute independently.

\item \textbf{Identity bias is data-driven but not uniform in mechanism.} Masking
the identity token erases it for \texttt{toxic-bert} and the SVM - a clean lexical
shortcut - but \emph{increases} it for our generic classifier, so a mitigation
validated on one model cannot be assumed to transfer to another.

\item \textbf{Compression preserved fairness but cost robustness.} The soft-KD
student is the fairest model we measured - identity FPR $0.008$, an order of
magnitude below its own teacher's $0.087$ - which rejects H2 on the axis H2 was
about, and does so even from an early-stopped checkpoint trained on a tenth of
the intended data. But the same student is more than twice as easy to evade as
its teacher (leetspeak $0.280 \to 0.634$), landing beside the bag-of-words
baseline. Distillation transferred the teacher's clean-text decision boundary and
its fairness, not its resistance to obfuscation: the KD loss only ever sees
unperturbed comments. Compression is not free, but the price is paid somewhere
other than where H2 predicted.

\item \textbf{``Robust'' is not one property, and reporting one direction
flatters the model.} \texttt{toxic-bert} is the hardest model to evade and
simultaneously among the easiest to trick into false alarms: the same
perturbations that fail to hide real toxicity flip neutral comments to ``toxic''
$65$-$91\%$ of the time, against an $11\%$ baseline. The SVM shows the exact
mirror image. Any single robustness number hides this.


\end{enumerate}

comment contains an identity term, since our results show that is precisely where
false positives concentrate, plus the model's measured per-group error rates so they
can calibrate how far to trust it. Affected identity groups need transparency at the
population rather than the individual level: published per-group false-positive
rates, the composition of the set on which they were measured, and a statement of
what mitigation was attempted and what it cost.

\textbf{2c. Who is responsible for each explanation.} The platform deploying the model owns the
user-facing notice and the appeals process, because it makes the enforcement decision
and holds the relationship with the user. The ML team owns moderator-facing
documentation - model cards, per-group error rates, known failure modes - because
only it can produce those numbers honestly. Public reporting is jointly owned: the ML
team generates the audit, the platform is accountable for publishing it and acting on
what it shows.

\textbf{3a. Our reflection on the AI output.}
\newline To begin with, we believe that the AI point-of-view is quite narrow to the problem itself, and there are many more stakeholders - these algorithms and identifying hate-speech can be used in many more places, not online forums, such as law-enforcers, researchers who want to track changes to minorities in some environment (real life and online), real-time tools for censorship and many more. Also, for each responsibility I would make sure to add a moderation, as long as I don't hurt the freedom-of-speech (which is even protected in the United States First Amendment). Also, to make it more ethical, we believe that the prompts to Claude response should be to think wider than the specific project problem, to tackle not what kind of stakeholder should behave and be aware, but how each side's rights clashes and how should the algorithm resolve these conflicts.
